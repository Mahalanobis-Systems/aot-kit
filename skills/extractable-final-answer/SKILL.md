## What it is

Put the final answer in one marked place after the reasoning, read it with a parser you have checked, and when a run hits its limit, spend a reserved last step on the answer and label it as forced. It pays wherever a parser or grader reads the answer: an instructed \boxed{} answer scored 93.9% where pattern-matching free text scored 20.8% on the same questions and model, answer-first JSON took GPT-4o from 95.60% to 55.34% on GSM8K, a nine-token answer cue held inside a 2,048-token budget raised two small thinking models by 8.0 and 7.0 points, and human review found 5.45 to 8.49 points of GPT-5.5 web-agent successes that a strict grader had rejected. Formats still cost models working near their limits (a long JSON schema took Claude Haiku 4.5 from 88.7% to 52.5% on hard math, and a forced submit-tool call took GPT-4o-mini from 60% to 10%), which reasoning first and formatting second mostly recovered. The limits: no study here tests a forced answer at an agent's turn cap (in one audit no run that hit the action limit had finished its task), the forced-answer results come from small models on math, and lenient parsers and unlabeled fallback answers both hide failures.

## When it applies

- **In the code:** no answer-format statement, or one shown only as a bracketed placeholder or an example value the model may copy; a structured answer schema with the answer field before the reasoning; a submit tool forced on the first call, with no room to reason before it; extraction that assumes a marker, or falls back to any matching letter or number anywhere in the text; string matching with no number, unit or markup normalization; no forced answer, and no budget held back for one, when the loop or the generation ends on a budget; harness fallback answers written without a label saying where they came from
- **In traces:** runs ending with no extractable answer (none, an echoed placeholder, output cut off before the answer marker, a turn cap reached); refusals or explanations returned as answers; numbers in the wrong format or wrapped in markup; runs ending at the limit with a correct value in context; runs ended by a provider or harness error that still report a final answer; forced or fallback answers not marked as such; parse rates that differ sharply between models on the same prompt
- **In eval results:** failures where the working was right but the submitted answer was not (instruction-grader-mismatch); a gap between strict and lenient scores, between the deterministic scorer and a symbolic or model-based equivalence check, or between an agent benchmark's automatic score and a human-corrected one
- **Probes:** Solved cases whose answer the harness may fail to read: output cut off at the limit, answers in equivalent forms, and each candidate answer format.

## How (outline)

1. Start from a baseline that separates wrong answers from unreadable ones: score a run as is and report the parse rate beside accuracy, then list runs with no extractable answer (none at all, a refusal, an echoed placeholder, output cut off before the answer marker, a loop that ended at its turn cap) and runs whose working holds the right value while the returned answer does not; in one public corpus of failed multi-agent runs, 23 of 95 ended with no answer at all (a stall, a cut-off output or an exhausted budget). Re-score a sample with a symbolic or model-based equivalence check, and for agents read the failed runs: a deterministic MATH-500 scorer rejected 3.3% (free text) to 10.5% (Markdown) of responses an LLM judge accepted, with 0.1% the other way, in another study a computer-algebra grader turned roughly 28% of string-graded MATH rows from wrong to right, and human review of GPT-5.5 on WebArena-Lite recovered 5.45 to 8.49 points of success the automatic evaluator had rejected (answers in a strict format, completed states a string rule missed, valid results followed by an invalid stop action).
2. State the format once, in the prompt's format section (3.a.iv): one marker the harness reads (\boxed{}, 'Final answer:' or a named field), placed at the end of the response, and the allowed values by name ('one of A, B, C, D') rather than a bracketed placeholder or an example value. On identical questions and model, an instructed \boxed{} answer scored 93.9% where pattern-matching the bare question's free text scored 20.8%; an example letter from another alphabet made one model answer in that alphabet (58.2%, last place) until the prompt named the letters (83.9%, 100% parse rate), and in another study a '#### <answer>' placeholder was echoed verbatim and scored as wrong.
3. Keep the reasoning ahead of the format: let the model work in free text or a thinking phase and commit to the answer at the end, and in a JSON or XML answer put the reasoning fields before the answer field (answer-first JSON took GPT-4o from 95.60% to 55.34% on GSM8K; answer-first XML left two small models at 10.98%). If a format request still costs accuracy, as it did for every open-weight model in one study and for closed models on tasks near their limits in another (a 1,774-character JSON schema took Claude Haiku 4.5 from 88.7% to 52.5% on hard math while Claude Sonnet 4.6 held 88.7% against 89.3%, and Claude Opus 4.7 lost 5.3 points on AIME, not significant on its own), answer freely first and convert the answer to the format in a second call: that significantly improved 42 of 72 comparisons (6.8 points on average) and significantly worsened 2; reasoning first and formatting second within one response recovered 80% (Haiku) and 87% (GPT-4o-mini) of the lost accuracy; and a thinking phase significantly improved 43 comparisons and worsened 11 (thinking settings are 1.b.i). If the answer is submitted through a tool, leave room to reason before the call: forcing the tool call took GPT-4o-mini from 60% to 10% on 100 hard math problems because it emitted only the arguments, while submitting through the tool in a second round held 59%. Constrained decoding guarantees the shape (format compliance 55.7% to 92.2%) but not the answer (accuracy 57.3% without it, 55.7% with it, 61.5% free-form), so apply it to the final answer after the reasoning, not to the whole response.
4. Harden extraction: strip markup, normalize numbers and units, compare by value, take the last marked answer, and record refusals, empty answers and echoed placeholders as their own outcomes rather than as wrong answers. Do not let a lenient fallback fill the gaps unseen: a leaderboard that read the marker where present and any option letter otherwise scored models that ignored the format leniently and compliant ones strictly, and fixing it moved 185 of 215 published cells; keep strict and lenient (or raw and corrected) scores side by side.
5. Hold back room for the answer inside the budget and force it when the loop or the generation reaches that reserve: give the model its work so far and ask only for the answer in the stated format (for example by closing the thinking block and appending 'Final answer: \boxed{', or 'Therefore, my final answer is'), constrained to the valid answers where they can be listed. With 64 of 2,048 output tokens held back, a nine-token cue of that kind turned every empty endpoint into an answer in the main run and raised two small thinking models by 8.0 and 7.0 points on 100 GSM8K questions (7.0 and 10.0 on a repeat) at under 1% change in tokens and wall time, and it beat reading the cut-off output with a lenient parser (90 and 87 correct against 84 and 81); in an earlier small-model study a forced continuation recovered correct answers on 63.5% of no-answer rows in one cell, against 19.2% for a full revision turn, and constrained re-extraction made every answer parseable. Count forced answers apart from correct ones: 6 and 5 of the newly answered questions were readable but wrong. In an agent, hold back the last turn the same way, but expect it to return partial work: in the WebArena-Lite audit none of 40 failed GPT-5.5 runs that used up a 25-action budget had completed the task (mean verified progress 56.2%). Raising the limit is not the fix (sizing it is 7.b.iii): doubling a generation cap from 8,192 to 16,384 tokens cut unparsed responses only from 15.9% to 14.0%, though where a long format is what fills the output a higher cap does help (Claude Haiku 4.5 under JSON: 52.5% at 2,048 tokens, 84.0% at 8,192).
6. Record where each returned answer came from (the agent's own answer, a forced answer at the limit, a harness fallback, or none) and report runs the harness ended apart from agent errors: in one public corpus of multi-agent failures, a provider's content filter killed the orchestrator in 26 of 58 runs and the experiment script then printed a final answer in 25 of them that no agent had produced, which the corpus blamed on another agent in 21.
7. Measure on the same cases, per model, before and after: accuracy under one stated protocol, parse rate, no-answer, refusal and malformed-answer rates, strict against lenient score, and answers that flip from right to wrong when the format changes; for agents, also how each run ended (answered, forced at the limit, harness fallback, crash) and the accuracy of each group. Format defects are model-specific: the same placeholder left 828 of one model's answers on one subject unparsed (827 of them real answers in the wrong alphabet) and cost another model 17 of 60,389 responses.

## How to measure

- **before-after** (Tier 2): same cases and model, before and after: accuracy under one stated extraction protocol, parse rate, and the rates of no answer, refusal, echoed placeholder, malformed answer and answer cut off by a limit; score strictly (the stated format) and leniently (any recognizable answer) side by side, and re-score a sample with a symbolic or model-based equivalence check (for agents, by reading failed runs); when the answer format or submission path changes, run old and new on the same questions for each model and count answers that flip from right to wrong; for the forced answer, cut runs at the generation or turn budget and compare forcing the answer from a held-back reserve against returning nothing, counting forced answers that are readable but wrong apart from correct ones
- **monitor**: in production traces: parse rate and no-answer rate per model and prompt version (an outlier parse rate for one model on one prompt is a format defect, not a model result); refusals and explanations returned as answers; where each returned answer came from (agent, forced at the limit, harness fallback) and how often each is right; runs ended by a provider or harness error that still report an answer; and the share of answers read only by a fallback parser

## Expected impact

- **Quality:** improves, medium: an instructed \boxed{} answer against pattern-matched free text: 20.8% to 93.9% on one subject and model, and fixing a lenient fallback parser moved 185 of 215 leaderboard cells (Huang, Oct 2026); reasoning-first against answer-first JSON on GSM8K: 95.60% against 55.34% for GPT-4o, 80.21% against 31.01% for Llama-3.1-8B (Kumar, Oct 2026); a second formatting call significantly improved 42 of 72 open-weight comparisons, by 6.8 points on average, and a deterministic scorer rejected 3.3% to 10.5% of responses an LLM judge accepted (Lee, Apr 2026); a nine-token answer cue charged to a 2,048-token budget raised two small thinking models by 8.0 and 7.0 points on 100 GSM8K questions (Chang, Oct 2026), and a forced short continuation recovered correct answers on 63.5% of no-answer rows against 19.2% for a full revision, in one cell (Chen, Aug 2026); human review recovered 5.45 to 8.49 points of GPT-5.5 web-agent successes a strict automatic evaluator had rejected (Gan, Oct 2026); limits: none of 40 GPT-5.5 runs that used up a 25-action budget had finished the task (Gan, Oct 2026); a long JSON schema cost Claude Haiku 4.5 36.2 points on hard math and Claude Opus 4.7 5.3 points on AIME (not significant alone), Claude Sonnet 4.6 showed no significant loss (89.3% against 88.7%), and a forced tool call took GPT-4o-mini from 60% to 10% (Fan, Jun 2026), while three recent closed models, Claude Haiku 4.5 among them, paid little or no format tax on the benchmarks of another study (Lee, Apr 2026); Claude Sonnet 4.5 scored higher with answer-first JSON on GSM8K (96.13% against 87.79%) (Kumar, Oct 2026); and the format reminder alone was mixed on GPQA, train 0.54 to 0.60 and test 0.54 against a 0.61 baseline (VeRO, Feb 2026)
- **Cost:** degrades, small: stating the format and hardening extraction add nothing per run; converting the answer to the format in a second call means two inference calls instead of one (Lee, Apr 2026); the forced answer at the limit is one short continuation or call on the runs that need it (a 32-token continuation in Chen, Aug 2026), and held inside the budget a nine-token cue left total output tokens 0.63% and 0.60% below first-box stopping alone (Chang, Oct 2026)
- **Performance:** none: no added latency on runs that answer in the stated format; with the answer cue held inside a 2,048-token budget, request wall time was 0.80% and 0.71% shorter than first-box stopping alone in single-request runs (Chang, Oct 2026); the optional second formatting call, or a forced answer turn in an agent, adds one sequential call on the runs that use it, which no source here times
- **Governance:** mixed, small: a fallback answer that is not labeled hides harness failures: in 25 of 58 runs of one public multi-agent failure corpus a script printed a final answer that no agent had produced after a provider error, and the corpus blamed another agent for 21 of them (Wang, Nov 2026); keeping raw and corrected, or strict and lenient, scores side by side exposed 5.45 to 8.49 points of grader false negatives on a web-agent benchmark (Gan, Oct 2026) and a leaderboard parser whose fix moved 185 of 215 cells (Huang, Oct 2026)

## Caveats

Most of the evidence comes from single-call benchmarks: no study here measures a forced final-answer turn at an agent's turn cap, the forced-answer results are small models on math (an exploratory probe on models up to 9B whose fallback parser was added after inspecting failures, and a nine-token cue on two 4B and 8B thinking models over 100 GSM8K questions, where 6 and 5 of the newly answered questions were still wrong), and in the one agent audit none of the 40 GPT-5.5 runs that hit the action limit had finished the task. Format costs depend on the model and on how hard the task is for it: Claude Haiku 4.5 paid little or no format tax in one study but lost 36.2 points on hard math in another (mostly output cut off at 2,048 tokens), Claude Opus 4.7 lost 5.3 points on AIME (not significant on its own) while Claude Sonnet 4.6 showed no significant loss, Claude Sonnet 4.5 scored higher with answer-first JSON on GSM8K (96.13% against 87.79%), and a thinking phase made 11 of 72 comparisons significantly worse. The largest extraction effect (20.8% to 93.9%) is one subject and one model and changes the prompt as well as the parser, and the agent grader audit is one rollout per task on 165 tasks, with corrections that mix answer formatting and missed state changes. The governance result comes from one corpus of released multi-agent traces, and in VeRO the format reminder alone was mixed on GPQA (train 0.54 to 0.60, test 0.54 against a 0.61 baseline).
