## What it is

Let a cheap model do the work and call an expensive one only when a trigger fires: a cascade escalates when the cheap answer looks unreliable or fails a check, a router picks the model before the call, and an advisor or critic brings a stronger model in mid-task. It pays when the trigger really separates the cheap model's failures: two-model cascades beat the best single model at the same budget by 2.1 to 8.2 points on average across budgets, a learned consult policy recovered 44% to 81% of the gap to the strong model on three agent benchmarks at 34.5% to 48.1% of its cost, and a small grader's stated confidence sent 7% of decisions to the large model for 76% lower cost and 61% lower median latency (kappa 0.802 vs 0.819). Speed gains need a cheap model that is actually faster and rare escalation: a strong model reviewing a 4B model's drafts only at state-changing actions came within a point of it on two customer-service benchmarks in 41% to 54% of its wall time, but with cloud models on MCP-Atlas it scored 8.9 to 12.9 points lower with no time saved. The trigger is the weak link: none of six commercial routers beat a random mix of two well-chosen models at matched cost, escalating a web agent's least self-consistent actions did worse than escalating at random, a third to fifth model in a cascade added at most 0.43 points, and in Anthropic's tests the advisor bought about what more effort does (1.7 points for 2.1 times the cost on coding).

## When it applies

- **In the code:** Two models with an escalation call from worker to oracle.
- **In traces:** Escalation or consult rate and when it fires (turn of the first consult, share in the last third of the run); the trigger score's distribution (a stated confidence stuck on a few values cannot separate failures); strong-model share of tokens and cost; median and p95 wall time of escalated against kept tasks; escalated tasks whose outcome changed; advice the worker then ignored.
- **In eval results:** Success, cost and wall time per completed task against each model alone across effort settings, against random escalation at the same rate and against a random mix of the two at the same spend; step-level switching run live, not scored by replaying logged trajectories.
- **Judge:** Whether escalations were warranted.
- **Probes:** Tasks the worker solves, tasks only the oracle solves, and tasks neither solves.

## How (outline)

1. Start from single-model baselines on your own tasks: the cheap and the strong model alone, each across effort settings (the strong one at low effort too), priced per completed task and timed (median and p95), plus a random mix of the two at the same spend. A pairing must beat that whole curve: Anthropic's coding advisor pairing (90.1% at $2.92 per attempt) landed on its executor's own effort curve (88.4% at $1.38 on high, 91.1% at $4.11 on xhigh), and no commercial router beat the random mix. Picking one model per call site statically is 1.b.i.
2. If the outcome is checkable (tests, a schema, a sound verifier), try the simplest cascade first: run cheap and escalate only the failures. Opus 5.5 at low effort with its failures re-run at high solved about 97% for about $0.17 a task, against 95.3% for $0.29 with everything at high, a figure computed task by task from existing runs rather than run end to end. A checker that passes bad work lets those failures through, so audit it on a gold sample (9.c.iii).
3. Use two models, not a ladder: allowing three to five models did not raise mean test accuracy in 118 of 135 comparisons (at most +0.43 points), and even with true query difficulty known a two-model roster was within 1 point of the best roster of any size. Take the cheap model from the cost-accuracy frontier; in one 26-model set it trailed the strongest by 1.6 points at a 96% discount.
4. Measure the trigger before trusting it, against escalating at random at the same rate and cost. For a confidence-score cascade, check how well the score separates the cheap model's errors from the strong model's: log-probability scores reached AUROC 0.63 to 0.80 for the cheap model's correctness yet beat random escalation at the same escalation rate (not the same cost) by only about 0.3 points on MATH, and in a computer-use agent token-probability uncertainty predicted subgoal failure at AUROC 0.488 to 0.540. A confidence the cheap model states in its answer (a 0 to 100 field after its verdict, no extra call) can work if it varies: in one grading task Claude Haiku 4.5's (AUROC 0.857, 13 distinct values) sent 7% of decisions to Claude Opus 4.6 for kappa 0.802 against 0.819 for Opus alone, while Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite's took 3 values and its cascade could not close the gap at any threshold. Do not assume sample agreement works in agents: escalating a web-shopping agent's least self-consistent actions succeeded less often than escalating at random (4.5% against 16.0% at a 20% escalation budget, 41.0% against 50.5% at 60%), while a probe trained on where the weak and strong models' actions differ beat random (21.0% and 76.5%). For a pre-call router, check it against the random two-model mix at the same spend (commercial routers trailed it by 1.0 to 10.5 points).
5. In agents, decide inside the run on execution evidence and workflow phase rather than up front or on the agent's own sense of being stuck, and cap consults. On OSWorld, escalating on runtime uncertainty scored 71.65% against 59.92% for routing at the start (not cost-matched). On SWE-bench Verified, a learned policy at 2.78 consults per episode solved 48.0%, fixed triggers (before committing to an approach, before finishing, on stalls) 41.3%, random placement at similar volume 28.0%, and the orchestrating model left to choose (not volume-matched) made 0.41 consults per episode, 77% of them in the last third; on Terminal-Bench the learned policy only tied the fixed triggers (34.7% each). If a model decides when to escalate, make it a separate, capable judge: on Terminal-Bench a GPT-5.4-mini judge reached 71.91% where the 9B worker judging itself reached 50.56% (cloud model alone 74.16%). Once a critic hands control to the strong model, keep it there until the critic clears a higher return threshold, and give both models one shared record of verified progress and recent failures: an embodied agent run this way stayed within 5 points of the cloud model alone while the cloud made 24% to 34% of its action requests, beat random and periodic hand-offs on tasks the local model failed alone at 30% to 50% cloud use (88.4% to 93.0% against 74.4% to 79.0%; no difference at about 20%), and resumed 20 recorded hand-off states successfully every time with the shared record against 25% without it. Where latency matters, let the cheap model draft and run read-only calls, hold its first state-changing action for the strong model to review in one pass, accept the longest valid prefix by meaning and repair the first bad step: within 1 point of the strong model on tau2-Bench in 41% to 54% of its wall time, while exact-match review dropped Retail to 82.5%; in coding, cloud help on write and edit steps tracked success most. Do not re-run whole agent tasks on the strong model after a failed self-check: that cascade cost $59.68 against $45.90 for the strong model alone.
6. Watch the consult rate and whether the advice is used. Sonnet 5.5 at its default effort never consulted on 20 SWE-bench Pro problems; a pairing that consulted on most tasks at default effort consulted on few once its effort was lowered, and then scored below the executor alone; with the advisor tool attached but never called, GPQA cost 12% to 25% more (its definition is about 1,000 prompt tokens per request); ignored advice made up 58% of the learned policy's remaining SWE-bench failures. A consult that fires on most tasks can cost more than the strong model alone (1.8 times on chart reading), and escalating on the weak model's own progress check made more strong-model calls than the strong model alone (18.5 against 13.8 per airline task) and took longer (385.2 s against 305.2 s).
7. Measure on held-out tasks with gold outcomes over several seeds: success, cost per completed task (counting failed cheap attempts, strong-model tokens and the context resent with each review or consult) and median and p95 wall time against every step-1 baseline, plus escalation or consult rate; re-run at every model or price change. Run step-level switching live, not by stitching logged runs: after an early model swap in one SWE-bench pilot only 3% to 8% of the states a replay would score still occurred, and replay missed all 5 success-relevant outcomes. Expect a speed gain only if the cheap model is faster than the strong one: with a local 4B model about as slow as hosted ones, wall time did not fall, and reviewed drafts raised compute by 30% to 87%.

## How to measure

- **experiment** (Tier 4): paired eval on held-out tasks with gold outcomes, 3 or more seeds: the escalation setup against the cheap and the strong model alone across effort settings, against random escalation at the same rate and against a random mix of the two at the same spend; success, cost per completed task counting failed cheap attempts, strong-model tokens and the context resent with each review or consult, escalation or consult rate, and median and p95 wall time; run step-level switching live or by branched rollouts, not by replaying logged trajectories
- **monitor**: escalation or consult rate by task type and when it fires, the spread and drift of the trigger score (a stated confidence stuck on a few values cannot separate failures), strong-model share of tokens and cost, p95 latency of escalated tasks, success of escalated against kept tasks, advice the worker ignored, and true error on a gold-labeled audit sample the trigger never sees (9.c.iii); repeat the experiment at each model or price change

## Caveats

The agent results are small, single-run or the authors' own methods: the learned consult policy used one Gemini pair on 40 to 50 held-out tasks with 3 seeds (success standard deviations up to 7.2 points) and outcome-screened development tasks, the critic study tuned its thresholds on the evaluation instances and ran once in simulation, the hidden-state trigger ran once on 262 OSWorld tasks and needs an open-weight worker, and Anthropic's advisor figures are internal, sometimes single runs on Claude models, from a living page whose August 2026 revision rated the advisor more favorably (closing 60% to 90% of the gap). Trigger evidence is thin and mixed: stated confidence was tested on one grading task with no random baseline, self-consistency lost to random in one web-shopping agent whose strong model was a task-trained policy, the cascade study tested only log-probability and trained scores on older open and GPT-4o-class models, and the router study is single-turn with a random pair picked from the frontier with hindsight. Speed gains need a faster cheap model and rare escalation (no wall-time gain with a local 4B model as slow as hosted ones, compute up 30% to 87%), and step-level switching scored by replaying logged runs is unreliable (one pilot on small open models). FrugalGPT's 98% is its best of three 2023 classification datasets (59.2% and 73.3% on the other two), and a cheap verifier used as the trigger can hide most of the cascade's errors, so measure true error outside it (9.c.iii).
